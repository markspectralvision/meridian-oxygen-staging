How does a PSA oxygen generator make medical oxygen from room air?

Pressure swing adsorption separates nitrogen from compressed air and leaves oxygen behind. The process has supplied hospitals for decades. Here is what happens inside the cabinet, and what the fire code asks of it.

The process

What happens between the air intake and your oxygen pipeline?

Four stages of oxygen production technology, repeated every few seconds, for as long as the system has power. This is PSA technology, the same oxygen generation technology behind every medical concentrator.

Compress and clean

An oxygen compressor stage draws room air, filters it, dries it, and removes oil and particulate. Feed air quality decides sieve life, so pretreatment is part of the system, never an accessory.

Adsorb nitrogen

Compressed air enters a bed of zeolite. Under pressure the zeolite holds nitrogen and lets oxygen pass. That is pressure swing adsorption, the same physics behind every oxygen concentrator.

Swing and regenerate

Pressure drops, the bed releases its nitrogen to vent, and the second bed takes over. Two beds alternate, so oxygen production never pauses while a bed regenerates.

Store, verify, deliver

Compressed oxygen fills an ASME receiver at storage pressure. An oxygen purity analyzer reads it continuously. Regulated gas leaves at pipeline pressure through the required filter and check valve into your oxygen distribution system, the oxygen distribution network and oxygen pipeline systems that serve as your central oxygen supply.

Purity, measured

What does the code require of an oxygen concentrator supply?

NFPA 99 has set requirements for oxygen concentrator central supply units since its 2018 edition. Your facilities director will recognise the list. Every item is in the specification, and every item is an oxygen safety measure written into oxygen safety standards for a reason.

Product gas meets Oxygen 93 USP or Oxygen USP
An oxygen sensor that reads concentration continuously and alarms below 91 percent, locally and at the master panel
A 0.1 micron filter rated for oxygen service at the outlet, a check valve, and an outlet valve with manual and automatic actuation
A vent valve sized for testing and validation, and a sample port near the analyzer
A second supply source. A concentrator never stands alone as a central supply

Margin, designed in

Why we specify above 93 percent

The Oxygen 93 monograph allows 90 to 96 percent. The master alarm trips at 91. A system that runs at a nominal 93 leaves one point of margin above a building-wide alarm. Meridian systems are specified to run higher in the band, so a warm plant room or a filter due for service shows up on a trend line, not on the master panel at three in the morning.

Purity drift is the first symptom of every common fault: a clogged intake filter, a dryer past its dew point, a leaking valve seat. Watching oxygen purity levels closely is how the system tells you it needs attention before your patients do. That is why every Meridian system ships with oxygen monitoring that your service contract reads remotely.

Clinical limits, stated first

Two things Oxygen 93 cannot do

Blenders that specify 99 percent

Some air-oxygen blenders and mixers are specified for oxygen of at least 99 percent, and the achievable fraction of inspired oxygen from a 93 percent source tops out near 0.93. Your respiratory therapy lead should review blender specifications before the design meeting, and we supply the list of questions to ask.

Low-flow anaesthesia circuits

Argon makes up a few percent of Oxygen 93 and can accumulate in a closed or minimal-flow anaesthesia circuit. The published guidance is fresh gas flow at or above twice oxygen consumption, and periodic venting of closed circuits. Anaesthesia should be consulted early, not informed late.
